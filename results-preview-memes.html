United States

Overall summary

Your article largely asks whether American force can change Iran’s behavior at an acceptable cost to Americans, leaving less room to ask whether Washington must change its own conduct. Making household prices and electoral risks more concrete than Iranian civilian suffering encourages readers to judge urgency through domestic consequences rather than the harms imposed abroad. Reliance on a former U.S. security official reinforces that starting point: bombing becomes a bargaining tool whose usefulness receives more scrutiny than its justification. Although your article acknowledges that the United States and Israel began the war, its explanation of renewed fighting places the immediate burden on Iran to stop obstructing shipping and overcome leaders’ resistance to compromise. Oman’s and Saudi Arabia’s coverage exposes a different possibility: cooperation may depend on American blockade relief and reliable commitments, not simply greater Iranian willingness to yield. Oman’s mediation role and Saudi Arabia’s reliance on maritime exports help explain attention to reciprocal restraint, including restrictions imposed by Washington, a Saudi security partner. That premise makes American conduct part of what needs correcting, without establishing that blockade and attacks are legally equivalent. Iran’s state broadcaster goes further by presenting Iranian violence as defensive; highlighting casualties serves Tehran’s interests but does not itself discredit the harm. Israeli coverage’s acknowledgment of civilian injuries also shows that recognizing suffering need not imply accepting Iran’s account. Together, these contrasts reveal a narrowed choice: how Washington should apply pressure receives more attention than whether reducing it could protect civilians and enable agreement.

Michael Singh describes efforts to “negotiate with words and also to negotiate with force.” His question, “Do they seem more eager to return to talks?”, makes Iran's response a test of bombing's usefulness.
